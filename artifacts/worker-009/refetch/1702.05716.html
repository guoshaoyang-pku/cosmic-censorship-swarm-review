Strong cosmic censorship in spherical symmetry for two-ended asymptotically flat initial data II. The exterior of the black hole region

Jonathan Luk Address: Department of Mathematics, Stanford University, Palo Alto, CA, USA Email address: jluk@stanford.edu and Sung-Jin Oh Address: Korea Institute for Advanced Study, Seoul, Korea Email address: sjoh@kias.re.kr
Abstract.

This is the second and last paper of a two-part series in which we prove the C2C^{2}-formulation of the strong cosmic censorship conjecture for the Einstein–Maxwell–(real)–scalar–field system in spherical symmetry for two-ended asymptotically flat data. In the first paper, we showed that the maximal globally hyperbolic future development of an admissible asymptotially flat Cauchy initial data set is C2C^{2}-future-inextendible provided that an L2L^{2}-averaged (inverse) polynomial lower bound for the derivative of the scalar field holds along each horizon. In this paper, we show that this lower bound is indeed satisfied for solutions arising from a generic set of Cauchy initial data. Roughly speaking, the generic set is open with respect to a (weighted) C1C^{1} topology and is dense with respect to a (weighted) C∞C^{\infty} topology. The proof of the theorem is based on extensions of the ideas in our previous work on the linear instability of Reissner–Nordström Cauchy horizon, as well as a new large data asymptotic stability result which gives good decay estimates for the difference of the radiation fields for small perturbations of an arbitrary solution.

We emphasize that in the above scheme, the lower bound along the event horizon is completely generated by back-scattering near the asymptotically flat end. In particular, the lower bound does not occur due to some polynomial tail of the initial data. Rather, it is generated by the interaction between the scalar field and the geometry of the spacetime.

In particular, the stability result quantifies the “effect” of small perturbations of a particular solution, which is important for showing that the generic set of initial data is open and dense in appropriate topologies. 1.1. Ingredients of the proof

In this subsection, we give a high-level overview of the structure of the proof of Theorem 1.3. First, our proof relies heavily on the following known analytic results:

  1. (1)

    (Dafermos–Rodnianski Price’s law [15]) In a seminal work, Dafermos–Rodnianski [15] showed that for any (potentially large) spherically symmetric solutions to (1.1) such that the event horizon asymptotes to a subextremal1212 12 Recall that this holds in our setting due to an observation of Kommemi, see Proposition 3.7. event horizon, then in an appropriately normalized double null coordinate system, the scalar field ϕ\phi, together with its first derivatives, decay with a quantitative inverse polynomial rate (see Section 5 for a precise statement). One can infer from this, as we show in this paper, that in an appropriately normalized gauge, appropriate geometric quantities also approach the corresponding values of a Reissner–Nordström spacetime with a quantitative rate.

  2. (2)

    (Linear instability [27]) In [27], we proved that for the linear wave equation □gR​N​ϕ=0\Box_{g_{RN}}\phi=0 on a fixed Reissner–Nordström background, solutions arising from generic smooth and compactly supported initial data fail to be in Wl​o​c1,2W^{1,2}_{loc} at the Cauchy horizon. One key step of the proof in [27] was to establish an L2L^{2}-averaged lower bound on the event horizon similar to that in (1.2) (see Theorem 1.5 for a precise statement of this linear result).

The rough program is therefore to use the Price’s law decay result to establish that at least after a sufficiently long time, the spacetime is sufficiently close to Reissner–Nordström and therefore one can apply (a suitable modification) of the linear instability argument in [27] to show that the lower bound required in Theorem 1.2 holds for solutions arising from generic data.

The argument in [27] is based on analysis of the quantity 𝔏\mathfrak{L} and it consists of two parts: namely, (1) show that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 implies that the lower bound on the event horizon is achieved, and (2) show that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 is a generic condition. In the nonlinear setting, using the Price’s law decay (with appropriate extensions), part (1) can indeed be carried out. That is, it can be shown that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 implies the desired lower bound. Moreover, this is achieved with a similar proof as in [27] except that now we also have to handle some decaying error term. More precisely, we use a contradiction argument to show that if the lower bound on the event horizon is not satisfied, then one can propagate decay estimates which are sufficiently strong to contradict 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0.

However, the genericity of 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 is much more subtle in the nonlinear setting. To begin with, in the linear setting, genericity amounts to proving (1) 𝔏\mathfrak{L} is bounded in terms of some initial norm of the scalar field, and (2) the existence of a single solution such that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. This immediately implies that the generic set is open1313 13 In fact, this statement is so obvious in the linear setting that it was not explicitly stated in [27]! and the non-generic set has co-dimension at least 11. In the nonlinear setting, however, one at least needs to understand the perturbations of an arbitrary large data solution.

It is quite fortunate that both the openness of the generic set and the nontrivial co-dimensionality of the non-generic set turn out to rely on the same main technical result, which is a stability statement for the quantity 𝔏\mathfrak{L} for perturbations of any (potentially large data) solution. To prove this stability result, we need to compare two nearby solutions and prove that their difference remains small and decays sufficiently fast. For this, it is important to carefully choose a future-normalized gauge (for each solution) in order to control the scalar field, the geometric quantities and their differences. To control the difference of the scalar fields, we rely on many ideas developed in recent years on linear wave equation on black hole spacetimes, in particular the Dafermos–Rodnianski rpr^{p}-weighted estimates [17]; see Section 1.2.2 for further discussions.

It is obvious that the stability result implies the openness of 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. However, it is a bit more subtle to see how the stability result is useful for showing that 𝔏=0\mathfrak{L}=0 is unstable. A simpler form of the key idea is already used in [27] to construct a solution with 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. Roughly speaking, given a solution with 𝔏=0\mathfrak{L}=0, one constructs a one-parameter family of perturbations (parametrized by ϵ\epsilon) of such that the perturbation of the scalar field is compactly supported near the asymptotically flat end. The initial perturbation can be chosen such that

  1. (1)

    it gives an ϵ\epsilon-perturbation of 𝔏\mathfrak{L} near spatial infinity, to the past of an outgoing null hypersurface {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\};

  2. (2)

    the perturbation of the spacetime is essentially supported to the past of {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\} in the sense that the deviation of the solution with the background on {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\} is of size ≪ϵ\ll\epsilon.

The key point is that this can be achieved just by analyzing the region to the past of {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\}, without having to track the global dynamics of the perturbated spacetime. Nevertheless, once this is achieved, one can rely on the stability result of 𝔏\mathfrak{L} to conclude that the contribution to 𝔏\mathfrak{L} to the future of {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\} is much smaller than the main term to the past of {u=Up​e​r​t}\{u=U_{pert}\}. Let us recap by outlining the main steps of the proof. We include here a pointer to the more precise statements of these steps, which can be found in Section 4, after the necessary notations are introduced.

  1. (1)

    Step 1, Theorem 4.1: Relating the condition on the the event horizon to 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. To derive this result, we prove the contrapositive: We assume that the L2L^{2}-averaged lower bound fails and this can be viewed as an upper bound which we can then propagated all the way up to null infinity and show that we must have 𝔏=0\mathfrak{L}=0. This argument, which was introduced in [27], is very robust and the decay estimates in [15] is more than sufficient to show that this also applies in the nonlinear setting.

  2. (2)

    Step 2, Theorem 4.2: Stability theorem in the exterior region. This step is the technical heart of this paper. We prove that for every fixed solution arising from admissible data, ϵ\epsilon-size perturbations of the initial data lead to C​ϵC\epsilon-size perturbations (with appropriate weights) of the radiation field along future null infinity, with CC depending only on the fixed solution.

  3. (3)

    Step 3: Openness of 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. Once the stability theorem is proved, the openness of 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 follows as an immediate corollary.

  4. (4)

    Step 4, Theorem 4.3: Instability of 𝔏=0\mathfrak{L}=0. To show the instability of the condition 𝔏=0\mathfrak{L}=0, we construct a one-parameter family of perturbations of the background data such that the difference of the scalar fields behaves in a similar manner as for the linear case [27]. As described in the paragraph above, we construct perturbations so that the main term near spatial infinity can be well-controlled and such that the remaining contribution can be shown to be small error terms using Step 2 above.

1.2. Previous works on the stability of black hole exteriors

We will not review all the relevant literature regarding the general study of the strong cosmic censorship conjecture for asymptotically flat initial data in spherical symmetry or the instability of Cauchy horizons. For our particular problem at hand, the most relevant previous results by Dafermos [9, 11, 12], Dafermos–Rodnianski [15] and Kommemi [24, 25] have already been discussed in other parts of this paper. For a further discussion of the relevant literature, we refer the reader to our companion paper [26].

Instead, in this and the next subsections, we will review some works related to the analysis of the black hole exterior region. In this subsection, we in particular focus on stability results, in the spherically symmetric or in the linear setting. We will also take the opportunity to point out the relevance of these works to our present paper.

1.2.1. Stability of black hole exterior regions in spherically symmetric problems

In spherical symmetry, the study of black hole exterior regions is much simpler than outside spherical symmetry since the area radius function is strictly positive and the (monotonic) Hawking mass is coercive and subcritical. In particular, for a large class of physical matter models including (1.1), Dafermos showed that in the presence of a trapped surface, null infinity is complete [10]. Roughly speaking, this implies that even for large data, as long as the presence of a trapped surface is guaranteed, the solution has a “regularly behaved” exterior region. Using moreover the monotonicity of the Hawking mass, it can be easily shown that the explicit Schwarzschild, Reissner–Nordström black hole exteriors are orbitally stable. Similar results also hold for the vacuum equations in (4+1)(4+1)-dimensions under triaxial Bianchi-IX symmetry [13].

Even in spherical symmetry, the question of asymptotic stability is considerably harder than that of orbital stability. In the setting1414 14 See also [23] for a closely related problem on the asymptotic stability of the Schwarzschild—Tangherlini family of solutions for the vacuum equations in (4+1)(4+1)-dimensions under triaxial Bianchi-IX symmetry. of (1.1), since (by a generalization of Birkhoff’s theorem) all spherically symmetric asymptotically flat solutions in the absence of the scalar field are given by Reissner–Nordström, asymptotic stability of Reissner–Nordström can be captured by the decay rate of the scalar field. This was achieved in the remarkable work of Dafermos–Rodnianski [15], who proved inverse polynomial pointwise decay upper bound estimates for the scalar field, which are consistent with the decay rate suggested by the heuristic study of Price [36]. Even more remarkably, the result in [15] holds for large data solutions as long as a trapped surface is present! See Section 5.3 for the precise statement of the result in [15] and further discussions of its consequences. As mentioned before, in the context of this paper, the result in [15] is an important starting point of the analysis, since it gives very strong asymptotic control of any solution arising from admissible initial data.

Finally, let us note that in the analysis of this paper, we also prove an asymptotic stability result, for perturbations around any fixed (potentially large data) solution. We show that the difference of the scalar fields between the perturbed solution and the background solution decays. However, in contrast to [15], in our setting there are no gauge-invariant ways to measure the difference between two solutions with non-vanishing scalar fields, and the proof therefore requires a careful choice of gauges normalized in the future for each solution. In fact, one can easily convince oneself that for some unwise choices of coordinate system, the stability result no longer holds.

1.2.2. Boundedness and decay estimates for solutions to the linear wave equation in black hole exterior regions

Another line of works which is relevant to our approach in this paper concerns the study of the linear scalar wave equation

□g​ϕ=0\Box_{g}\phi=0 (1.3)

in the black hole exterior region with a fixed background metric. This problem has received a lot of attention in the past decade, see [3, 4, 1, 5, 18, 19, 16, 17, 21, 14, 29, 31, 38, 39] and the references therein for a sample of results. A culmination of these results is a theorem of Dafermos–Rodnianski–Shlapentokh-Rothman, which proves that for all subextremal Kerr spacetimes, solutions to (1.3) (arising from sufficiently regular initial data) obey both energy decay and pointwise decay estimates.

A reason that the study of (1.3) is relevant to our paper is that (as mentioned in Section 1.1) a crucial ingredient of the proof is to show that small perturbations of any (potentially large) data lead to globally small perturbations of the solution. In order to prove such a result, one needs to control the long time behavior of the difference of two solutions. The main contribution in the equations for the difference comes from the linear terms, which can be controlled using methods from the study of (1.3).

A particularly relevant technique is the Dafermos–Rodnianski rpr^{p}-weighted estimates [17] (see also recent extensions by Moschidis [33]). This is a physical space technique of proving decay estimates on a very general class of asymptotically flat spacetimes, and it has been previously implemented for nonlinear applications [40]. This method plays a crucial role in one of the steps in the stability argument. We will defer a more detailed discussion to Section 8.

1.3. Previous works on lower bounds for solutions to the linear wave equation in black hole exteriors

Perhaps even more relevant to the present paper than the stability results in the previous subsection are theorems on lower bounds for solutions to linear wave equations. Those results should be thought of as the analogues of the lower bound (1.2) (which is proven in Theorem 1.3) but in a linear setting. There are in the literature three different approaches to obtain such lower bounds, which we will review in the next few subsubsections. This in particular includes the work [27], to be described in Section 1.3.2, which forms part of basis of the proof of Theorem 1.3. After reviewing the relevant literature, in Section 1.3.4, we will comment on the choice in this paper to attack the nonlinear problem using the approach of [27].

1.3.1. Lower bound via time-translation symmetry

The simplest result that can be proven regarding lower bounds for solutions to linear wave equations would be one such that an inverse polynomial lower bound is imposed in the initial data. Though this is not explicitly available in the literature, using the methods of Dafermos–Shlapentokh-Rothman [20] (see also McNamara [30]), it can be proven by using the time-translation symmetry of the spacetime and the scattering theory developed in [21] that

Theorem 1.4 (Dafermos–Shlapentokh-Rothman).

Generic smooth and spherically symmetric initial data with a (fixed but otherwise arbitrarily fast) inverse polynomial rate to the linear wave equation □gR​N​ϕ=0\Box_{g_{RN}}\phi=0 on a fixed Reissner–Nordström spacetime with parameters satisfying 0<|𝐞|<M0<|{\bf e}|<M give rise to solutions ϕ\phi which obey the following lower bound along the event horizon:

∫ℋ+vα​(∂vϕ)2​𝑑v=∞\int_{\mathcal{H}^{+}}v^{\alpha}(\partial_{v}\phi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v=\infty

for some sufficiently large α\alpha, where vv is the Eddington–Finkelstein coordinate defined by v=12​(t+r∗)v=\frac{1}{2}(t+r^{*}) with r∗=r+(M+2​M2−𝐞22​M2−𝐞2)​log⁡(r−r+)+(M−2​M2−𝐞22​M2−𝐞2)​log⁡(r−r−)r^{*}=r+(M+\frac{2M^{2}-{\bf e}^{2}}{2\sqrt{M^{2}-{\bf e}^{2}}})\log(r-r_{+})+(M-\frac{2M^{2}-{\bf e}^{2}}{2\sqrt{M^{2}-{\bf e}^{2}}})\log(r-r_{-}).

The idea underlying this result is very simple: Consider a smooth and compactly supported initial data set on past null infinity such that the solution is non-trivial on the future event horizon. Such an initial data set exists by considerations in [21]. If this solution does not already obey the estimate in Theorem 1.4, then one can sum a series of solutions arising from time-translating and rescaling the initial data on past null infinity. This new solution will then satisfy the estimate in Theorem 1.4. Notice that this construction requires the possibility that the data on past null infinity only decay polynomially, which as a consequence implies that the data on a Cauchy hypersurface also only decay polynomially.

The advantage of this approach, as we see above, is that it requires very little information about the specific form of the metric. Thus, the result generalizes easily to Kerr and Kerr-Newman spacetimes. On the other hand, the solution that is constructed has a very special profile. Though in the linear setting, this is already sufficient to deduce genericity of solutions with a lower bound on the event horizon, it is difficult even to study stability properties of these solutions.

1.3.2. Lower bound via a contradiction argument in the exterior region

A second approach of establishing lower bounds for solutions to the linear wave equation on Reissner–Nordström was given as a part of our previous work [27] on the linear instability of the Reissner–Nordström Cauchy horizon. We will only state the result for spherically symmetric solutions (in particular in view of their special relevance to the present paper), but it of course follows easily that the following bound holds for the spherical mean of any (potentially non-spherically symmetric) solutions.

Theorem 1.5 (Luk–Oh [27]).

Generic smooth, compactly supported and spherically symmetric initial data to the linear wave equation □gR​N​ϕ=0\Box_{g_{RN}}\phi=0 on a fixed Reissner–Nordström spacetime with parameters satisfying 0<|𝐞|<M0<|{\bf e}|<M give rise to solutions ϕ\phi which obey the following lower bound along the event horizon:

∫ℋ+vα​(∂vϕ)2​𝑑v=∞\int_{\mathcal{H}^{+}}v^{\alpha}(\partial_{v}\phi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v=\infty

for any α>7\alpha>7, where vv is as in Theorem 1.4.

As discussed in Section 1.1, Theorem 1.5 is proven by identifying a quantity 𝔏\mathfrak{L} at infinity which is generically nonvanishing and such that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 implies the desired lower bound. In addition to the fact that Theorem 1.5 now proves a lower bound even for compactly supported initial data, the approach of Theorem 1.5 has the additional advantage that 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 is a stable property.

The proof of Theorem 1.5 proceeds by a contradiction argument: it is shown that if ∫ℋ+vα​(∂vϕ)2​𝑑v\int_{\mathcal{H}^{+}}v^{\alpha}(\partial_{v}\phi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v is finite, then one can obtain strong enough decay estimates in the exterior region which are inconsistent with 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0. As we have already indicated earlier, this theorem (or more precisely its proof) will play an important role in the present paper. This is in part due to the robustness of the proof, see Section 1.3.4. On the other hand, partly because the proof is based on a contradiction argument, the result is relatively weak. For instance, it does not give any lower bounds away from the event horizon.

1.3.3. Lower bound via precise late-time asymptotics

A third approach of obtaining lower bound has very recently been introduced, which moreover gives a good description of the leading order asymptotics. In particular, it is shown that a pointwise lower bound holds true everywhere in the black hole exterior:

Theorem 1.6 (Angelopoulos–Aretakis–Gajic [2]).

Generic smooth and compactly supported initial data to the linear wave equation □gR​N​ϕ=0\Box_{g_{RN}}\phi=0 on a fixed Reissner–Nordström spacetime with parameters satisfying 0<|𝐞|<M0<|{\bf e}|<M give rise to solutions ϕ\phi which obey the following pointwise lower bound

|∂tkϕ|​(u,v)≥ck​v−3−k(ck>0)|\partial_{t}^{k}\phi|(u,v)\geq c_{k}v^{-3-k}\qquad(c_{k}>0)

in the region {r+≤r≤R}\{r_{+}\leq r\leq R\} for any finite but fixed R>r+R>r_{+} and k≥1k\geq 1, where vv and r+r_{+} are as in Theorem 1.4.

The bounds in Theorem 1.6 imply a fortiori that ∂vϕ\partial_{v}\phi (on the event horizon ∂v\partial_{v} is parallel to ∂t\partial_{t}) obeys the estimates on the event horizon in Theorems 1.4 and 1.5. Combining with the ideas in [11], the results in [2] also give an alternative proof of the linear instability result of the Reissner–Nordström Cauchy horizon in [27]. This result combines sharp upper bounds of the solutions together with a clever application of the conservation of the Newman–Penrose quantities. In particular, it requires very precise upper bounds of the solutions, which are in turn derived using an extension of the Dafermos–Rodnianski rpr^{p}-weighted estimates.

1.3.4. Comments on the nonlinear problem

In view of the three philosophically distinct approaches to obtaining lower bounds, one can in principle attempt to obtain instability results for the nonlinear problem using any of these approaches. In this subsection, we explain our choice of using ideas in [27] to tackle the nonlinear problem.

First, simply because Theorem 1.4 constructs solutions to the linear wave equation with an L2L^{2}-averaged lower bound, Theorem 1.4 could in principle1515 15 Here, let us suppress the additional complication that (1.2) is in fact needed for a specific range of α\alpha. yield a result on the existence of perturbations of Reissner–Nordström data such that (1.2) holds. This then implies the instability of Reissner–Nordström Cauchy horizon. However, it is not immediately clear from the approach of Theorem 1.4 whether the L2L^{2}-average polynomial lower bound is a stable property. In particular, it would be more difficult to use this to prove a genericity statement or even to obtain an instability result for (potentially existing) large data solutions with a C2C^{2}-regular Cauchy horizon.

In contrast, at least in the linear setting, the other two approaches both prove that the lower bound is a stable property. The approach of Theorem 1.6 moreover has the advantage that the type of pointwise lower bound for |∂tϕ||\partial_{t}\phi| along the event horizon has a long history in the study of black hole interiors. In fact, that type of lower bound has long been assumed in the study of the instability of the Cauchy horizon, beginning with the pioneering works [22, 34, 35] on the Einstein–null dust model. For (1.1) in spherical symmetry, Dafermos showed in [11] that a pointwise lower bound for |∂tϕ||\partial_{t}\phi| along the event horizon as in Theorem 1.6 imply that the Hawking mass blows up at the Cauchy horizon and thus the spacetime is C2C^{2}-future-inextendible. It would therefore be an interesting problem to prove this pointwise lower bound in the nonlinear setting for generic Cauchy data, completing the program of Dafermos.

However, even in the linear setting, the proof of Theorem 1.6 requires first proving precise upper bounds. This in particular include the sharp Price’s law bounds [36] for the derivatives of the scalar field, and these are stronger than the known estimates for the nonlinear problem. Moreover, the method in [2] requires quite heavily on the staticity of the spacetime metric, which would require some extensions in order to be applicable to the nonlinear problem.

In contrast, the proof of Theorem 1.5 is very robust, and has the advantage that the L2L^{2}-averaged lower bound on the event horizon can be obtained without proving sharp decay upper bound estimates. To see this, recall from Section 1.1 that we need to show that (1) 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 implies the desired L2L^{2}-averaged lower bound and (2) 𝔏≠0\mathfrak{L}\neq 0 is generic. For (1), the ideas in [26] in fact need very little geometric information, see discussions in Section 6. In particular, the decay results in [15] are more than sufficient for this purpose. While the proof of (2) is considerably more involved, it should be noted that statements about 𝔏\mathfrak{L} are morally at the level of the L1L^{1} norm of the radiation field along future null infinity, which is again weaker than expected sharp decay of the radiation field1616 16 More precisely, it is known [15] that the radiation field Φ\Phi (cf. Definition 3.8) decays like |Φ|​(u)=O​(u−2)|\Phi|(u)=O(u^{-2}), while in our proof we only need to show that under small perturbations, the difference of the radiation fields decays with a rate =O⁡(ϵ​u−β)=O(\epsilon u^{-\beta}) for some β>1\beta>1 where ϵ\epsilon is the size of the initial data difference..

5. Consequences of Price’s law decay

In this section, we derive decay estimates in the exterior region of the maximal globally hyperbolic future development of an arbitrary admissible Cauchy data set. The estimates are based on the remarkable theorem of Dafermos–Rodnianski [15] on Price’s law, which provides quantitative decay rates (towards timelike infinity) of the scalar field in the maximal globally hyperbolic future development of the characteristic initial value problem. In order to apply the Dafermos–Rodnianski theorem, we need to first prove decay estimates near spacelike infinity to reduce a Cauchy problem to a characteristic initial value problem. After stating the results in [15], we also derive various ramifications of Price’s law, including in particular decay estimates for the geometric quantities (in an appropriate gauge) and for higher derivatives of the scalar field, which will play a fundamental role in the remainder of the paper. In the process, we introduce the null coordinates U,VU,V (referred to as initial-data-normalized) and u,vu,v (referred to as future-normalized), and discuss the different purposes they serve in the subsequent analysis.

This section is structured as follows:

  • •

    Section 5.1. We begin with the introduction of the (U,V)(U,V), (u,V)(u,V) and (u,v)(u,v) coordinate systems. UU and VV are normalized with respect to the (Cauchy) initial data, whereas uu and vv are normalized with respect to the future null infinity and the event horizon respectively.

  • •

    Section 5.2. We prove some bounds in a neighborhood of spacelike infinity that are necessary to justify the application of the results in [15] in our setting. We work in the initial-data-normalized null coordinates (U,V)(U,V).

  • •

    Section 5.3. We cite the theorem of Dafermos–Rodnianski (Theorem 5.5) and discuss some immediate consequences of it (Corollaries 5.6, 5.8 and 5.11). To measure the decay towards timelike infinity in a large rr region, the future-normalized (retarded) null coordinate uu is used.

  • •

    Section 5.4. We prove bounds on fundamental geometric quantities using Price’s law (Proposition 5.12). In order that the geometric quantities decay, we will use the future-normalized (retarded and advanced) null coordinates (u,v)(u,v).
